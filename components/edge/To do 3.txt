**5. Brief description of the research problem including references (300 words max) – references not included in word count.**  

*Nature/Gravity & Impact of the Problem:*  
The rapid global and local adoption of residential rooftop solar systems introduces severe technical challenges to legacy distribution networks. During peak sunlight hours, household solar generation often surpasses local demand, forcing electricity to flow backward into the low-voltage distribution transformer. This phenomenon, known as Reverse Power Flow, triggers dangerous voltage spikes exceeding standard regulatory limits (e.g., 240V). If unmanaged, these spikes cause localized hardware degradation, premature transformer burnouts, and frequent inverter tripping, leading to economic losses for utility providers and consumers.  

*User Persona:*  
The future users of this solution include electrical utility operators (such as the Ceylon Electricity Board - CEB) managing grid stability, and residential solar prosumers seeking stable financial returns on their renewable energy investments without facing aggressive, uncoordinated system shutdowns.  

*Research Cluster & Industry Vertical:*  
This project aligns directly with the Smart Systems and Cyber-Physical Networks research cluster, targeting the Renewable Energy and Smart Grid Utilities industry vertical. Attainment of Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs): This research directly advances SDG 7 (Affordable and Clean Energy) by enabling high-density solar integration, SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure) through the creation of resilient, low-cost smart grid frameworks, and SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities) by fostering localized sustainable energy production.

**6. Brief description of Existing Research and Systems (300 words max)**  

Current industry practices rely heavily on Centralized Optimal Power Flow (OPF) systems or rigid, localized communication-free droop settings (e.g., Volt-Var/Volt-Watt controls) pre-programmed into individual solar inverters. Academically, modern research focuses on deploying complex cloud-driven Machine Learning architectures to forecast generation patterns and schedule grid adjustments. From a communications perspective, Software-Defined Networks (SDN) and standard MQTT protocol layers are frequently evaluated to move smart meter telemetry packets under steady network environments.  

*Gaps/Limitations Identified:*  
Existing methodologies suffer from four critical limitations:  
1. Unfair Local Enforcement: Traditional model-free localized active power curtailment (APC) disproportionately penalizes prosumers located at the far end of the feeder lines, forcing them to throttle down more solar than households closer to the transformer.  
2. Static Network Rules: Standard smart grid communication architectures rely on generic QoS rules that remain static, failing to adapt network security parameters or routing priorities dynamically when physical electrical nodes enter high-voltage crises.  
3. High Operational Latency: Centralized or offline Hosting Capacity Estimation (HCE) models cannot calculate allowable PV exports fast enough to respond to cloud-induced, sub-second solar drops, demanding heavy communication infrastructure that is economically non-viable for developing regions.  
4. Cloud Cost Inefficiencies: Heavy cloud-edge frameworks naively stream massive volumes of high-frequency IoT data to central servers, incurring unsustainable cloud storage and bandwidth costs without considering grid state urgency.

**7. Brief description of the solution's nature, including a conceptual diagram (maximum 500 words).**  

*Addressing the Gaps:*  
This project presents an integrated, low-cost cyber-physical infrastructure designed to manage high-density solar integration safely and fairly without requiring expensive grid infrastructure upgrades. By implementing an optimized Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL) testing model, we bridge real physical hardware with automated local intelligence to solve the structural gaps of unfair curtailment, static communication protocols, slow cloud computational delays, and cloud data bloat.  

*System Perspective of the Solution:*  
- **Input Layer:**  
  - Physical: Real-time instantaneous analog voltage ($V$) and current ($I$) waveforms captured via non-invasive hardware sensors at a physical lab bench.  
  - Virtual: High-fidelity consumption and generation smart meter time-series datasets (Pecan Street Dataport) representing a 50-household neighborhood, combined with live NREL weather API variables (solar irradiation, ambient temperature, cloud cover).  

- **Process Layer:**  
  - Embedded Node: Applies an on-chip Kalman filter to clean up AC noise and executes an algorithmic fairness curtailment loop.  
  - Network Engine: Runs an adaptive MQTT broker that activates lightweight ASCON encryption and upgrades message routing streams to high-priority QoS pathways only when a physical voltage limit is breached.  
  - Edge Intelligence Node: Hosts a lightweight Machine Learning model (Random Forest/XGBoost) within an optimized Docker microservice to approximate heavy grid equations and estimate neighborhood hosting capacity.  
  - Cloud Analytics Node: Executes a deep LSTM network for 24-hour look-ahead solar forecasting and processes a grid-aware data-tiering compression logic.  

- **Output Layer:**  
  - Dynamic, localized, and fair active solar throttling control commands sent back to the microcontroller node.  
  - Real-time web monitoring dashboards (Grafana/Node-RED) displaying compressed operational insights and macro-level forecasts for utility operators.

Abstract: Recently, many countries have focused on generating 
greener energy. As a result, the number of solar photovoltaic (PV) 
systems connected to the low voltage network has shown a rapid 
increase around the world. Many studies are being carried out to 
analyze the potential impact of high penetration of solar PV on 
the operation and performance of electricity distribution systems. 
This study proposes a Monte Carlo based approach to evaluate the 
impacts of rooftop solar PV on low voltage networks and a case 
study is presented for a typical unbalanced residential network 
in Sri Lanka using a three phase, four wire model. The model 
has been validated by comparing the load flow simulation results

The impacts were analyzed using parameters such as voltage 
unbalances, neutral currents, voltage and thermal limits. Results

levels due to the violation of some of the parameters. Further how 
phase balancing could be used to minimize high neutral currents

Traditionally, power systems have been designed

Monte Carlo Implementation 
In this study, the number of PV installations connecting 
to the distribution grid was selected by considering the 
number of customers and feeders of the network. Customer 
unit loading and PV system data were provided as the 
input variables for the simulation. In order to investigate 
the impact of PV penetration in future, a large number of 
PV deployment scenarios were obtained by varying the 
capacity and the location of PV installations using a Monte 
Carlo based stochastic framework. It is a nondeterministic 
numerical method of approximately solving problems using 
sequence of random numbers. The computation procedure 
of the Monte Carlo approach is as follows, 
1. Select the distribution type of the random variable. 
2. Generate a sampling set from the distribution. 
3. Conduct simulations using the sampling set. 
For the implementation of Monte Carlo approach, 
random numbers were generated corresponding to PV 
capacity, nodes to which in PV plant will be connected 
and phases to which the panels will be connected. To 
carry out this step, the ‘rand’ function in MATLAB was 
employed and the numbers were sampled from the uniform 
distribution with a range of [0,1]. Power flow simulations 
were executed for unique PV deployment scenarios by 
varying PV capacity and location.

Case Study 
To adequately investigate the impacts of solar PV on a 
last mile distribution network, it is convenient to consider 
a real distribution network instead of a hypothetical 
network where the impacts are measurable through proper 
instrumentation. The proposed methodology presented in 
the previous section was applied to the residential network 
shown in Figure 4 which is situated in Dehiwala area and 
called Lotus Grove Housing Scheme. All required data 
of the distribution network was collected from the main 
Sri Lankan electricity provider, Ceylon Electricity Board

Domestic Loads 
In Sri Lanka, the annual demand profile varies where 
the maximum demand is approximately four times the 
minimum demand (Public Utilities Commission of Sri 
Lanka, 2017). This pattern was utilized to scale domestic 
loads in simulation. The average power consumption of 
a house (unit consumption) was taken as 0.5kW and a 
factor of 0.4 was used to reduce the peak load to minimal 
load. Thus, the maximum and the minimum unit loads 
were assumed as 1.0kW and 0.25kW respectively. Single 
phase loads were distributed among the three phases in 
order to ensure that the overall network is balanced or 
approximately balanced.

Pandapower provides identical numerical accuracy to MATLAB-based MATPOWER for balanced steady-state power flows because its core solver inherits and expands upon the same mathematical foundation, while OpenDSS yields higher precision for unbalanced distribution systems through specialized multi-phase component modeling. [1, 2, 3, 4]
Core Accuracy Comparison
•	Steady-State & Transmission Accuracy: Pandapower and MATPOWER (running in MATLAB) achieve virtually identical results for balanced three-phase AC power flow and optimal power flow calculations. This happens because pandapower originally leveraged a robust Python port of MATPOWER's Newton-Raphson numerical methods. [1, 2, 3, 4]
•	Distribution & Unbalanced System Accuracy: OpenDSS is considerably more accurate for detailed distribution feeder analysis, high penetration of distributed energy resources (DERs), and unbalanced multi-phase operations. While pandapower includes asymmetrical/three-phase extensions, OpenDSS models phase-to-ground coupling and specialized transformer winding configurations natively at a granular level. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]
•	Short-Circuit Calculation Accuracy: Pandapower calculates fault currents strictly according to the IEC 60909 standard, making it reliable for European-style standard industrial and grid compliance. MATLAB user-scripts or commercial toolsets often allow more customizable symmetrical component matrix manipulations depending on the specific implementation. [1]
Key Operational Trade-offs
•	Computation Speed: Pandapower executes iterative calculations faster than standard interpreted Python loops due to its use of the Numba just-in-time compiler, frequently rivaling or beating MATPOWER on larger network iterations. [1]
•	Time-Series Analysis: OpenDSS handles multi-rate, long-term quasi-static time-series simulations natively inside its engine. Pandapower can perform time-series, but it requires external pandas and Python scripting loops to manage the profile scheduling. [1]
•	Licensing Cost: Pandapower is completely free and open-source in Python, whereas MATPOWER requires a paid commercial MATLAB license (unless run via GNU Octave). [1]

Here is an honest breakdown of the learning curve and implementation difficulty for your specific project.
Why OpenDSS is slightly harder at the very beginning
•	Different syntax logic: Pandapower uses standard Python DataFrames (pp.create_bus, pp.create_line). It feels like normal data science. OpenDSS uses a custom text-based scripting language (DSS script commands). You pass these script strings from Python to the OpenDSS engine using libraries like opendssdirect.py or py_dss_interface. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]
•	Component definitions: In pandapower, you define a line by its length and standard impedance. In OpenDSS, because it is multi-phase, you must explicitly define line geometries, spacing, or complex phase-impedance matrices (LineCodes). [1, 2]
Why OpenDSS is actually EASIER for your specific project
Once you get past the first weekend of setting up the network, OpenDSS makes your specific research gaps much easier to code:
•	Modeling 50 Households with Pecan Street Data: In OpenDSS, you can define a Loadshape object using your time-series CSV data and attach it to a specific household meter with a single line of code. OpenDSS will automatically step through the time-series. In pandapower, you have to write custom Python for-loops to manually update the load values at every single timestamp, which easily bugs out and slows down execution. [1, 2, 3]
•	Modeling Solar Inverters (Volt-Watt / Volt-Var): OpenDSS has built-in smart inverter objects (InvControl). You can program standard droop curves natively to simulate "traditional" curtailment. This gives you an immediate baseline to compare against your novel "Adaptive Cyber-Physical Edge" fairness algorithm. Doing this in pandapower requires complex custom optimization scripts. [1, 2, 3]
•	Phase Imbalance: Residential solar grids are rarely balanced. If House 1 is on Phase A and House 2 is on Phase B, OpenDSS handles this out-of-the-box (Bus1=NodeName.1). Pandapower is fundamentally built for balanced, single-phase equivalent transmission systems; forcing it to accurately do unbalanced low-voltage modeling is a massive headache. [1, 2, 3, 4]
